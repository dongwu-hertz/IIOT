About This Document
This is a detailed user guide for the acoustic camera, covering the complete process from product introduction to installation and operation. It helps field personnel use the XS-SCF05 to perform the following three types of detection tasks:
Partial Discharge Detection (Discharge Detection Version): captures ultrasonic signals generated by discharges in electrical equipment and locates potential faults in switchgear, transformers, cable joints, etc.; the console includes a PRPD (Phase-Resolved Partial Discharge) plot.
Gas Leak Detection (Leakage Version): detects turbulent ultrasonic noise generated by gas leakage from pressurized pipelines (independent of gas type); supports leakage-cost estimation.

Product Introduction
A mini acoustic camera, also known as an acoustic imager, converts sound-field information into a visual image. Its core technology is based on a microphone array and beamforming algorithms. By analyzing the phase differences of sound waves arriving at different microphones, it accurately localizes the sound source and measures its intensity, ultimately generating an “acoustic image” in which color and brightness represent the sound-pressure distribution. The device integrates sound acquisition, image acquisition, and sound-source localization visualization in a compact, lightweight form factor that can be easily mounted on various inspection robots, giving them a “sense of hearing” for non-contact, visual diagnosis of equipment faults. Its detection band covers both audible sound and ultrasound, and it performs well for steady-state or transient sound sources as well as stationary or moving objects.
At present, the acoustic camera is mainly used in three core applications. The first is pressurized-gas leak detection, which is independent of the gas medium and can detect any type of leak. The second is partial-discharge detection in electrical equipment, which locates potential faults by capturing ultrasonic signals produced by discharge. The third is mechanical-noise detection, which captures abnormal bearing/gear friction and impact noise from rotating machinery such as motors, pumps, fans, and gearboxes, localizes abnormal-noise sources, and assists with early fault warning.

Detection Distance Assessment
In general, the best measurement performance is achieved when the acoustic camera is kept within 1–20 m of the object being tested.
Acoustic-camera localization and imaging are entirely based on the physical characteristics of sound propagation. In production environments, the sound-field conditions around the target must be considered comprehensively and detection positions should be arranged appropriately.

Factors Affecting Acoustic-Camera Detection
How does sound propagate? Vibration of the sound source causes alternating compression and rarefaction of surrounding air molecules, propagating outward as longitudinal waves. Sound waves carry energy, which continuously attenuates during propagation.
How does sound attenuate in the environment? Sound-wave energy decreases with distance due to the combined effect of three mechanisms:
1. Geometric spreading loss: as sound radiates outward, its energy spreads over a spherical surface. The sound pressure level decreases by approximately 6 dB each time the distance doubles (20×log₁₀(2)≈6).
2. Air-absorption loss: sound waves drive air molecules, and part of the energy is converted to heat through viscosity, thermal conduction, and molecular relaxation. Air absorption increases sharply with frequency (approximately proportional to the square of frequency). This is the key reason why different frequency bands propagate differently: high-frequency ultrasound attenuates much faster than low-frequency sound.
3. Obstacles, reflection, and scattering: on-site equipment, walls, and other objects can block, reflect, and scatter sound waves, changing their propagation paths and energy.
Characteristics of ultrasound: the human audible range is approximately 20 Hz–20 kHz; ultrasound above 20 kHz cannot be heard by the human ear. Ultrasound has a short wavelength, good directivity, weak diffraction, and rapid attenuation in air. This is why an acoustic camera can “see” small leaks, discharges, and abnormal noises that cannot be heard by the human ear.

What Determines the Maximum Detection Distance?
The maximum detection distance is not fixed; it depends on both the “detected signal” and the “environment.”

Imaging Frequency Band and Threshold Adjustment (Core Settings)

Imaging frequency band: adjustable from 2 to 65 kHz using integer values. The difference between the upper and lower limits cannot be greater than 20 kHz or less than 2 kHz, and the upper limit must be greater than the lower limit. After adjustment, the shaded area of the spectrum represents the currently selected band.
Imaging threshold: an energy threshold. When the ambient-energy value exceeds the threshold, the status changes to “Abnormal” and a sound-field image appears in the live view. The location of the sound-field image indicates the sound source (leak point/discharge source). A value of -30 is generally recommended so that detected sound can be imaged. If substantial interference exists in an industrial environment and needs to be filtered out, increase the threshold appropriately based on site conditions.

Practical tip: first use the spectrum to observe the dominant frequency of the target signal and set the upper and lower band limits around the target peak (bandwidth ≤20 kHz). Then set the threshold between the “ambient background energy” and the “target-signal energy,” so that the normal environment does not trigger an alarm while an abnormal condition produces imaging.
Frequency trade-off: high frequencies (for example, 50–65 kHz) provide high spatial resolution but strong air attenuation and short detection range, making them suitable for small nearby targets. Lower frequencies (10–30 kHz) attenuate less and support long-range inspection (30 m+). The “sweet spot” for most industrial faults is 20–50 kHz.

Introduction to Feature Attributes
Sound Pressure Level: A Measure of Sound “Intensity”
Definition: A physical quantity used to measure sound intensity, expressed in decibels (dB).

Formula:

Description: Lp is the sound pressure level (unit: decibel, dB); prms is the RMS value of the measured sound pressure (which can be understood as the average sound-pressure level); pref is the reference sound pressure. Internationally, the reference is defined as the weakest 1000 Hz pure-tone sound pressure audible to the human ear, i.e., 20 µPa (0.00002 Pa).
Dominant Frequency: The “Fingerprint” Feature of Sound
Definition: The frequency component in which the signal energy is most concentrated and the amplitude is highest in the frequency domain.

The dominant frequency is the frequency at which the energy of an acoustic signal is most concentrated in frequency-domain analysis. After the time-domain signal is converted to the frequency domain through a Fourier transform, the dominant frequency corresponds to the peak frequency in the spectrum. Frequency components near the dominant frequency carry most of the signal energy and largely determine the principal pitch or timbre of the sound. Therefore, dominant frequency is an important parameter for identifying sound-source characteristics and is widely used in mechanical fault diagnosis, speech recognition, environmental noise analysis, and other fields. Combining sound pressure level and dominant-frequency analysis provides a more complete description of the physical and perceptual characteristics of a sound, improving assessment accuracy and practical effectiveness.
Spectral Difference: A Measure of State “Deviation”
Definition: A quantified value representing the degree of similarity or difference between the spectrum of a segment of sound acquired in real time and a reference spectrum (i.e., the amount of change in the acoustic-signature components).
Purpose: To obtain useful information by analyzing similarities and differences between a signal and a target signal. Many signals are difficult to distinguish in the time domain (that is, from waveforms showing amplitude versus time), but their spectra have distinctive “fingerprint” characteristics. By comparing these “fingerprints,” signals can be identified, classified, diagnosed, and monitored.
Calculate the square root of the mean of the squared amplitude differences at corresponding frequency points. The smaller the acoustic-signature change value D, the closer the two spectra are.

Formula:
Description: D is the spectral cosine difference; fmax is the upper frequency limit used in the calculation; A1 is the amplitude of the frequency components of the reference signal (sample file); A2 is the amplitude of the frequency components of the measured sound. The smaller the spectral cosine difference, the more similar the spectral shape and distribution of the measured signal are to those of the reference signal.

Band Energy Ratio
Definition: Band Energy Ratio (BER) is a key signal-processing metric used to quantify the proportion of total signal energy contained within a specified frequency band. When signal energy is concentrated mainly in a particular band, BER quantifies this characteristic.

Purpose: By quantifying the proportion of signal energy within a specific frequency band, BER reveals distinctive features of the signal’s frequency-domain distribution, enabling accurate signal identification, classification, and condition monitoring. Many signals may exhibit similar waveforms in the time domain (such as vibration, noise, or electrical signals) and may therefore be difficult to distinguish directly. However, their frequency-domain energy distributions can differ significantly, particularly in key bands such as fault-characteristic frequencies or biological rhythm bands, where the degree of energy concentration acts like a “spectral fingerprint.” By calculating the ratio of energy in the target band to total energy, BER focuses on the contribution of these key bands and effectively extracts the signal’s core characteristics:
Formula:
Description: energy of the i-th frequency band; total signal energy; energy ratio of the i-th frequency band (expressed as a percentage); n: start value (lower limit); m: end value (upper limit).

Discharge Type Identification (Supported in Discharge Mode Only)
Purpose: Used to identify the discharge type. Supports corona discharge, floating discharge, and surface discharge.

Kurtosis (Supported in Mechanical Noise Detection Only)
Definition: Kurtosis is a statistical measure of the peakedness of the probability distribution of a real-valued random variable. It characterizes the height of the probability-density curve around its mean and, fundamentally, also reflects the heaviness of the distribution tails. A standard normal distribution (raw kurtosis = 3) is commonly used as the reference.

Purpose: In rotating-machinery noise monitoring, kurtosis is a highly sensitive indicator for early fault warning. The kurtosis of noise from equipment operating normally and steadily is typically around 3. When bearings or gears develop early pitting or wear, periodic impact pulses mixed into the signal can rapidly increase kurtosis to above 5, often well before abnormalities become apparent in conventional sound-level metrics. It can also be used with ISO 1999 to correct hearing-damage assessments for impulsive noise and avoid underestimating actual risk with conventional A-weighted sound levels.
Formula:
Description: μ4 is the fourth central moment, and σ is the standard deviation.

Crest Factor (Mechanical Noise Detection Only)
Definition: Also called the amplitude factor (crest factor), this is a physical quantity describing the waveform characteristics of a periodic signal. It is defined as the ratio of the waveform peak value to its root mean square (RMS) value, directly reflecting how high the signal peak is relative to the average energy level. A larger value indicates a higher proportion of sharp pulses in the waveform.

Purpose: In rotating-machinery noise monitoring, crest factor accurately quantifies the severity of fault impacts. Under normal operating conditions, the crest factor of equipment noise is typically in the range of 1.4–2. When faults such as rotor misalignment, imbalance, or bearing spalling occur, the value can rapidly rise above 3. It is also an important reference for sensor selection, helping prevent measurement distortion caused by high-peak impact signals and ensuring accurate acquisition of fault signals.

Formula:

Description: The numerator is the signal peak value, and the denominator is the signal RMS value.

Environmental Analysis Before Configuring an Alarm Strategy
Before configuring an alarm strategy, it is recommended to perform the following steps in the target detection environment:
1. Switch the device to single-detection mode.
2. Configure any temporary alarm strategy and perform multiple detections.
3. Record and statistically analyze the feature values obtained from each detection (such as sound pressure level, dominant frequency, band energy ratio, kurtosis, crest factor, etc.).
In a typical scenario, if the ambient sound pressure level and dominant frequency fluctuate only within a small range, these values can be used to establish the normal background baseline.
Alarm Decision Logic
When the device performs a detection task, alarm determination follows the following two-level decision process using a progressive two-stage validation mechanism:
1. Stable Imaging: first confirm that continuous, valid, and stable acoustic-imaging results exist in the current spatial environment (that is, continuously or persistently valid sound-source localization points).
2. Strategy-Condition Matching: if stable imaging is available, further determine whether the current detection data satisfy the trigger conditions configured in the enabled alarm strategy.

The final alarm output is equivalent to the logical expression: Alarm Output = Stable Imaging State is TRUE AND Strategy-Condition Match is TRUE. This fully follows the AND truth table and prevents false triggering caused by null or invalid data. In other words, an alarm is generated when a stable imaging point exists in the current spatial environment and the analyzed audio data satisfy the alarm strategy. If no stable imaging point exists, no alarm is generated even if the analyzed audio data satisfy the alarm strategy.

Mechanical Noise Monitoring Mode
Principle and Applicable Scenarios
When bearings, gears, and other components in rotating machinery (motors, pumps, fans, gearboxes, compressors) develop wear, pitting, cracks, poor lubrication, misalignment, or imbalance, they generate abnormal vibration and friction noise. Part of this energy is radiated as audible sound and part as ultrasound (friction/squeal and impacts). The XS-SCF05 frequency band of 2–65 kHz covers both the low-frequency and ultrasonic components of mechanical noise. Beamforming localizes the abnormal-noise source and overlays it on the visible-light image, enabling non-contact, long-distance “listen-and-locate” inspection.
Typical Applications:
Early bearing damage in motors/pumps/fans (inner race, outer race, rolling element, or cage faults)
Abnormal gearbox meshing (tooth-surface wear, broken teeth, sideband modulation)
Blade/rotor imbalance, shaft misalignment, and loose fasteners
Early faults in wind-turbine gearboxes and generator bearings, and blade cracks (crack propagation generates characteristic acoustic waves)
Advantages compared with vibration-based methods: ① non-contact—no sensor needs to be attached to the equipment; ② equally effective at low rotational speeds, where vibration methods often have poor signal-to-noise ratio; ③ multiple sound sources can be localized simultaneously; ④ suitable for mounting on inspection robots/robot dogs.

Operating Procedure
1. Log in and confirm that “Mechanical Abnormal-Noise Mode” is active.
2. Observe the spectrum on the console and set the imaging band around the dominant frequency of the abnormal noise.
3. Set a medium imaging threshold and fine-tune it after the machine stabilizes at idle/no-load or under load.
4. Basic settings: target count 1, low sensitivity, imaging enhancement on.
5. Acquire a healthy baseline: it is recommended to record a spectrum of the equipment in its “healthy state” as the reference for “Spectral Comparison.”
6. Configure and enable the alarm strategy → start detection.
7. Observe the acoustic image to locate the abnormal-noise point; evaluate the degree of deterioration together with the acquired result records.
8. Export records from the Data page and establish an equipment health file (acoustic image + spectrum + timestamp).

Safety and Maintenance
Default account: admin / 123456. Change the password after the first connection.
“Restore Initialization” resets the IP address and account password to their defaults and deletes all historical data. Use with caution.
Device logs can be downloaded as system_logs.log under System Configuration → Device Logs. Device Self-Test can be used to check whether the microphones are operating normally.
Operating temperature: -20°C to +55°C; humidity: 10%–95% RH, non-condensing. The enclosure is IP65 dust- and water-resistant but is not designed for immersion.
Firmware upgrades are performed under “System Upgrade.” Do not power off the device during the upgrade.
